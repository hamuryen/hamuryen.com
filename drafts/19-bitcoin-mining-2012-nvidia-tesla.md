# I Mined Bitcoin in 2012 on an NVIDIA Tesla GPU. Then a Hard Drive Died.

*The most expensive hard drive I never bought was the one I bought instead of Bitcoin.*

---

It was late 2012. I'd just started my first real engineering job. I was 22, excited about everything, and sitting in front of a Dell workstation with an NVIDIA Tesla GPU jammed inside it. The card was the size of a small country. No display output, no fan shroud, just raw compute. We used it for CUDA programming: face recognition, license plate detection, video analytics. Serious work. The kind of work where you compile something and go make tea.

One afternoon, a coworker walked up to my desk.

"Have you heard of Bitcoin?"

I had not.

He explained it to me like I was five, which was appropriate because my understanding of economics was roughly at that level. There's a program. You run it on your computer. It does math. The math earns you money. The money shows up on a website.

"How much money?"

"Bitcoin is around 11 dollars right now."

Eleven dollars. A movie ticket. A mediocre lunch. For doing math. On a computer I already had.

## The Experiment

We installed whatever mining software was popular at the time (I honestly cannot remember the name). Pointed it at the Tesla card. Hit start. The same GPU that spent its weekdays detecting faces in surveillance footage and reading license plates from blurry traffic cameras was now doing math for strangers on the internet. The workstation started humming in a way that made the facilities guy look over from his desk.

After a day or two, we checked the mining pool website. About 4 or 5 dollars had accumulated. We looked at each other with the grin of two guys who had just discovered free money.

"Let's leave it running over the weekend," my coworker said.

Brilliant plan. Forty-eight hours of uninterrupted GPU mining. We'd come back Monday to a small fortune. Or at least enough to buy lunch.

## Monday

We walked in Monday morning. The workstation was off.

Not sleeping. Not in standby. Off. Dead. The kind of off where you press the power button and nothing happens, then press it again harder as if force helps with electronics.

My first thought, and I'm not proud of this, was not about the work we might have lost. It was: "Please don't let the Tesla card be dead." That GPU was worth more than my monthly salary.

We opened the case. Tried different power configurations. Swapped cables. Nothing. Then we put in a spare hard drive, and the machine booted. The old disk had died. Probably already on its way out, and a weekend of continuous GPU mining at full load was the final push.

The Tesla was fine. The disk was toast. And with it, whatever Bitcoin we'd accumulated over the weekend.

## The $180 Fix

It was lunchtime. We walked to the nearest electronics store and bought a hard drive for about $180. Came back, installed the OS, restored everything, got the workstation back to its original state. Work machine first, mining experiment second.

Then I remembered: the mining pool. The website should still have our balance. We logged in.

Seven or eight dollars. That's what was sitting there.

I did the math that any reasonable person would do in that moment. We had just spent $180 on a hard drive. We had earned $7. The net result of our Bitcoin mining career was negative $173.

"This is stupid," I said. "We just lost money on free money."

We closed the browser tab. I never opened that website again. I don't even remember which mining pool it was.

## The Math I Wish I Hadn't Done

Bitcoin averaged around $11 in October 2012. Seven or eight dollars in the pool meant we were sitting on roughly 0.7 BTC.

We closed the tab on 0.7 Bitcoin.

But that's not the part that really stings. The part that stings is the $180.

If instead of buying a hard drive to mine Bitcoin, we had just bought Bitcoin with that $180, we'd have gotten around 16 BTC. Bitcoin has touched $140,000 since then. I'll let you do that multiplication yourself. I did it once, stared at the ceiling for a while, and decided to never do it again.

We had $180. We had heard of Bitcoin. We understood it was digital money. And we chose to spend $180 on a spinning metal disk so we could mine $7 worth of it.

As for that 0.7 BTC still sitting in whatever mining pool we used, I don't even remember the name. If you ran that pool in 2012 and somehow still have records of an NVIDIA Tesla that mined for four days in October, call me. I'll buy you a hard drive.

## The Guy Who Told Me About Bitcoin

My coworker who introduced me to Bitcoin? He didn't buy any either. Neither did anyone else in the office. We were all engineers working with NVIDIA Tesla GPUs, literally the best hardware for mining at the time. We had the tools, the knowledge, and the timing. We had everything except the imagination to take it seriously.

Because in 2012, Bitcoin wasn't an investment. It was a curiosity. Something a guy told you about at lunch. You tried it for a weekend, lost a hard drive, and moved on.

Nobody was wrong for not buying Bitcoin in 2012. Everyone had the same information and almost nobody acted on it. The few who did and held on were either visionary or just forgot their wallet passwords.

## What I Actually Took Away

I don't tell this story as an investment lesson. "You should have bought Bitcoin" is the most useless advice in history because it only works backwards.

But I do think about it when I see something new and dismiss it too quickly. We had the right timing. Bitcoin was $11. We were GPU engineers. The setup was perfect. What we got wrong wasn't the opportunity, it was the approach. We tried to extract value from it in the most literal, mechanical way possible (run program, get money) instead of stepping back and asking a bigger question: what does it mean that strangers on the internet are willing to pay $11 for something that only exists as math?

If we'd asked that question, maybe we wouldn't have closed the tab.

Or maybe we still would have. Eleven dollars for magic internet money sounded ridiculous in 2012. To be fair, it still kind of does. It just sounds ridiculous at a much higher price now.

The hard drive, by the way, worked great for years.
